As technology evolves, opponents are now using sophisticated methods like auto-reconnaissance, polymorphic malware, distributed denial of service (DDOS) attacks, credential stuffing and zero-day exploits, all of which are continually evolving to get around the standard security measures. This has prompted the need for security solutions to be able to self-configure, make intelligent decisions and to continuously learn about the environment to stay as secure as possible.Through this study, a new multi-agent cyber defense schema inspired by the behavior of a HoneyBee is introduced, which is a hybrid of machine learning, deep learning, reinforcement learning and adaptive deception techniques and presented as a part of the security architecture. It borrows from the collective intelligence of a HoneyBee colony to detect threatening activity and to ward it off, and it learns to analyse, mislead and neutralize malicious actions as they happen by numerous agents working together. The architecture integrates hybrid anomaly detection models to detect the suspicious actions, adaptive honeypot mechanisms to interact with and gather information from the person, and reinforcement learning-based decision engines for making decisions about acting autonomously.The outlined framework is a vicious circle of Detect-Reveal-Neutralize-Learn, that will make cyber security not only reactive, but adaptive and self-improving as well. Experimental tests on benchmark intrusion detection data show a high detection accuracy, a low number of false alarms, fast reaction time and resistance by detecting known and new attack patterns. Additionally, ability to mix deception with intelligence collection enables the system to evolve and enhance the defense system in a closed loop manner based on the returned information.Based on the results presented through this study, it can be concluded that BIA can be a potential solution for future generation cyber defense system, which is capable to handle the complexity and massive size of the actual cyber security threats while minimizing manual intervention in the cyber defense system to a maximum.KeywordsSimple strategies at the block level; simple approaches based on AI; simple approaches based on MAS; simple approaches based on RL; simple approaches using honey pot and IDS; simple approaches using swarm intelligence; HoneyBee Method; Adaptive Security; Cyber threat intelligence.IntroductionThe use of cloud computing and Internet of Things devices has become very common in areas, including government, businesses, healthcare and important infrastructure. Nowadays hackers do not just launch one attack they launch coordinated attacks that are designed to avoid being detected and to stay hidden in the system for a long time.In the ten years cyber threats have become more and more sophisticated. We see attacks, ransomware, phishing attacks that use artificial intelligence and big attacks that try to overwhelm the system. These threats use weaknesses in the technology and in the way people work making it very hard to defend against them.Traditional security measures like firewalls and intrusion detection systems are still good at stopping known threats. However they are not good at detecting changing attacks because they rely on known patterns. Since hackers are always changing their methods organizations need security systems that can adapt to threats. Recently artificial intelligence and machine learning have helped improve threat detection by finding behavior on the network Some models can even find patterns that are associated with cyberattacks.Some researchers are looking at how animals defend themselves for ideas on how to improve cybersecurity. Recognizing vulnerabilities and anticipating future changes.Identifying weaknesses and predicting future changes.With the rising number of cyber-attacks, researchers have started to shift their focus from signature-based security solutions. The initial IDSs were largely based on predefined attack patterns and rules that needed to be manually created. These systems were effective against known threats, but failed to detect new variations of attacks. With the evolution of cyber threats, focus started to gradually turn to smart detection methods that can identify abnormal behavior and not attack signatures.2.1 Machine Learning-Based DetectionMachine learning was among the first methods for threat detection that was intelligent. Techniques like Decision Trees, Random Forests, Support Vector Machines, and Bayesian Networks were shown to be capable of recognizing the suspicious traffic patterns in big data. Several studies showed enhancement in detection rates when compared to conventional systems. But as the network's behavior evolved over time, many machine learning models suffered from being less effective, and had to be retrained periodically to keep them accurate.2.2 Deep Learning ApproachesGiven the drawbacks of traditional machine learning, researchers turned to deep learning architectures that might be able to identify more intricate relationships within network traffic. Advanced attack patterns were effectively detected using models like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. Deep learning methods were found to be useful for enhancing the detection performance, especially with respect to multi-stage and unknown attacks. Although these benefits are obvious, there are also certain limitations in terms of computation complexity and lack of explainability.2.3 Reinforcement Learning in Security.Reinforcement Learning in Security.In recent years, the use of reinforcement learning has been studied as a means of learning to defend an autonomous system. Reinforcement Learning agents learn optimal actions from interacting with their environment, unlike the static rule-based systems. It can be used for attack containment, traffic filtering, dynamic access control, and automated incident response applications. While promising, current implementations are mainly response optimisation based and only offer partial integration with the wider defence architectures.2.4 Multi-Agent Defense SystemsMulti-agent cybersecurity models have been embraced due to the size of modern networks. Distributed agents can monitor various sections of a network, share intelligence and take coordinated defensive measures without centralized decision making. There are several systems inspired by swarms that have shown to be more scalable and resilient. Many proposed solutions focus only on the detection of anomalies, and don't include an ability to be deceptive or to learn continuously.2.5 Honeypots and Deception TechnologiesDeception technologies have increasingly been a part of cyber security because it gives the defenders a way to see attacker activity in a controlled environment. Honeypots and honeynets are important tools to gather threat intelligence and minimize exposure of critical assets. However, experienced attackers are likely to be able to detect static deception environments. This restriction has been a driving force in studying adaptive deception techniques that can change their strategy as a function of what is observed.2.6 A need for an integrated approach.In fact, extensive research has been conducted in the area of detection, response, deception, and distributed defense mechanisms, with significant strides being made. But these features tend to be developed individually as stand alone applications. There are few studies that integrate intelligent detection, adaptive deception, autonomous response, and continuous learning in one. Current solutions are largely designed for either threat detection, automated threat response or intelligence collection, adding to fractured security operations and slower response to threats. The current defence architectures are not very adaptive because there is not an integrated framework that can continually detect, mislead and neutralise cyberattacks and learn from them. So, the need for a single cyber defense framework: intelligent detection, adaptive deception, autonomous response and continual learning, all in a collaborative multi-agent system. Similarly, deception technologies often function as standalone tools without contributing to broader learning and decision-making processes.3.2 Research QuestionsCan a HoneyBee-inspired multi-agent architecture improve threat detection and response efficiency compared with conventional cyber defense approaches?How effectively can adaptive deception mechanisms contribute to threat intelligence collection and attacker engagement?Can reinforcement learning support autonomous response selection while reducing containment and mitigation time?Does continuous learning from attacker interactions improve the long-term adaptability of cyber defense systems?While existing solutions excel in isolation, there remains a critical gap in frameworks that autonomously link threat detection directly to adaptive deception for continuous, closed-loop learning.3.3 Research ObjectivesThe primary objective of this study is to develop a HoneyBee-inspired multi-agent cyber defense framework capable of integrating intelligent detection, adaptive deception, autonomous response, and continuous learning within a unified architecture.The specific objectives are:• To develop an intelligent threat detection mechanism using machine learning and deep learning techniques.• To design an adaptive deception layer capable of collecting meaningful threat intelligence.• To implement a reinforcement learning-based response mechanism for autonomous attack mitigation.• To enable collaboration among distributed security agents.• To establish a continuous learning process that improves future detection and response performance.3.4 Novel ContributionsCurrent cyber defence products tend to be built on either a single capacity, like intrusion detection, autonomous reaction, deception or threat intelligence collection, or not designed at all for an activity. IDS solutions based on machine learning are effective at detection, but not adaptive. Response selection is enhanced by reinforcement-learning-based methods, but there are no methods that have integrated deception capabilities. Likewise, honeypot based systems have been providing valuable information about the attackers but have not been effective at directly helping to optimize detection and response.The proposed framework distinctively from the existing ones, will combine intelligent detection, adaptive deception, autonomous response and continuous learning in a single multi-agent architecture. The framework is inspired by the HoneyBee colony behaviour enabling a distributed group of agents to share and collaborate in a continuous Detect–Mislead–Neutralise–Learn cycle, such that the threat intelligence they gather during an attacker engagement can directly impact future detection and response decisions.Unlike existing moving target defences that rely on predefined, static rules, the technical novelty of our HoneyBee Method lies in its shared Kafka-based event bus. This allows threat intelligence (the 'waggle dance') gathered dynamically by  deception agents to be fed in real-time as state updates to the Deep Q-Network, autonomously optimizing future response policies without central human orchestration.3.5 Threat ModelThe proposed framework assumes a distributed enterprise network of a user device, servers, cloud services, and critical business applications. Sensitive organizational information, network services, authentication systems and application servers are the main assets being protected.The threat model assumes that there are external and internal threats that can perform reconnaissance, brute force attacks, denial of service attacks, credential theft, web application attacks, deploy malware and attempt to escalate privileges. Attackers have some knowledge about the network services, but no access to the internal decision making processes of the defense framework. Defenders have been assumed to be able to observe network traffic, system logs and security events by using monitoring agents placed throughout the infrastructure. The framework presumes suspicious activities can be deflected to deception environments that prevent them from reaching critical assets. Detection agents detect threats, deception agents attack, response agents respond to mitigate, and learning agents continually learn defense policies based on learning.The boundary between layers is strictly enforced via Software-Defined Networking (SDN) rules, detection operates passively on mirrored traffic, deception operates in a strictly isolated sub-network (honeypot), and response actions (blocking/redirection) occur at the edge firewall level.3.6 Expected Contribution The proposed study is expected to make the cybersecurity field more resilient, self-learning and self-improving, with the continuous Detect–Mislead–Neutralize–Learn cycle, embedding machine learning, deep learning, reinforcement learning, and deception technologies. The architecture will focus on how interactions between attackers can be leveraged to provide valuable intelligence for the system and how that intelligence will enable the system to adapt and be more resilient to new attacks with less manual effort.4.The interaction of the Honey-bee with its environment is the subject of this talk.This talk is about How the Honey-bee inspired Architecture and Methodology!4.1 Design Philosophy5410203816350Figure  SEQ Figure \* ARABIC 1:multi-agent cybersecurity frameworkFigure  SEQ Figure \* ARABIC 1:multi-agent cybersecurity framework54102046736000Inspired by HoneyBee colonies, the framework uses multiple intelligent agents to collaboratively detect, share, and respond to cyber threats. The environment state space  consists of network traffic vectors and honeypot interaction logs. The distributed agents perform observations , extracting features to calculate a unified threat probability . The action space  spans . Agents communicate asynchronously by publishing intelligence payloads to a distributed messaging queue (e.g., Apache Kafka), creating a continuous feedback loop where action outcomes  directly update the state observations  for subsequent detection phases.4.2 Architectural Overview                                              The framework is composed of four types of functional agents: monitoring agents, detection agents, response agents and learning agents. Monitoring agents gather network traffic, logins, and system logs. Collected data is analysed by detection agents and threat-confidence scores are generated. Response agents identify appropriate mitigation measures such as monitoring, blocking or honeypot redirection. Learning agents use attack information and interaction logs from history to enhance their decision making for the future.Agent communication is done via shared threat-intelligence records and event notifications. Detection outputs go to response actions, and intelligence on the attackers' interaction is continually fed back into the learning process, forming a closed loop defence cycle.There are four types of specialist agents in the framework. Detection agents keep an eye on network traffic and deliver threat-confidence scores. An adaptive honeypot is run by deception agents who collect data from the attacker. Once a threat is detected, response agents determine the threat level, and begin to take mitigating measures such as monitoring, redirecting, isolating and blocking. Learning agents: analyse historical attack logs, interaction logs to fine-tune attack detection models and response policies. Agent communication is based on sharing of threat intelligence data and event notifications. 4.3 Detection LayerThe detection layer has a hybrid XGBoost and CNN-LSTM architecture. Network traffic and system logs are collected through  Kafka and processed before model inference. Data preprocessing includes removal of duplicate records, handling of incomplete entries, feature normalization, and transformation of categorical attributes. Extracted features include packet statistics, flow duration, protocol type, flag counts, login frequency, session behaviour, and temporal traffic patterns. XGBoost performs rapid classification of structured network-flow features, filtering a large proportion of benign traffic, while CNN-LSTM analyses spatial and temporal dependencies to identify complex and zero-day attack patterns. The outputs of both models are fused into a unified threat score that drives subsequent deception and response decisions. Continuous model updates are performed using validated intelligence gathered from honeypot interactions and newly labelled attack records.The XGBoost component was configured with a learning rate of 0.1, max depth of 6, and 100 estimators, functioning as a rapid first-stage classifier. The CNN-LSTM architecture utilizes 1D convolutional layers (64 filters, kernel size 3) to extract spatial features, followed by LSTM layers (50 units) to capture temporal session dependencies. Class imbalance in the CIC-IDS2017 dataset was mitigated using Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique . The models were trained using the optimizer matching according to the needs of application with early stopping configured at a patience of 10 epochs to prevent overfitting.4.4 Deception LayerThe deception layer abandons generic off-the-shelf honeypots in favor of a custom-built Application-Level Deception Engine. When the detection layer identifies malicious behavior, Software-Defined Networking (SDN) rules transparently redirect the attacker to an isolated Flask-based honeypot environment (Port 5001). To maintain a high degree of believability, the honeypot queries the real intelligence database in read-only mode and applies a deterministic column-wise derangement (permutation) algorithm. This ensures attackers are fed synthetically scrambled, yet structurally authentic, intelligence datasets without leaking actual sensitive values. All attacker interactions, search queries, and payloads are logged locally into a secluded SQLite database (honeypot_access), which is subsequently streamed via Apache Kafka back to the learning layer for continuous policy updates. 4.5 Response LayerThe response layer has a Deep Q-Network (DQN) agent to autonomously select mitigation actions based on the current threat context. The state space consists of threat score, attack category, historical behaviour, confidence level, and honeypot engagement status. The action space includes monitoring, traffic throttling, attacker redirection, IP blocking, and escalation for manual inspection.The reward mechanism is designed to encourage rapid threat containment while minimizing false positives and unnecessary service disruption. Positive rewards are assigned for successful containment and intelligence collection, whereas penalties are applied for delayed responses, incorrect blocking decisions, and excessive resource consumption. The DQN policy is updated through interaction with the environment and historical attack observations. To ensure operational safety, all response actions are validated against predefined security policies before execution. Learning part terminate when the threat is successfully contained, classified as benign, or exceeds a predefined observation window as per the adjusted value through the DQN model dynamically. The information collected in the detection and engagement processes as well as the information collected in the response outcomes are used to update the detection models and the response policies. The feedback process enables the system to evolve and continue to be effective in the face of dynamic attack methods. All security incidents become opportunities for system improvement in the learning process.4.7 Multi-Agent CoordinationThe framework coordinates four specialized agent types communicating via an Apache Kafka publish-subscribe protocol.Monitoring Agents: Input raw network packets; output normalized feature vectors to the shared memory queue.Detection Agents: Input feature vectors; apply the XGBoost-CNN-LSTM pipeline; output a unified threat confidence score.Deception Agents: Input redirected malicious sessions; output extracted attacker payloads, commands, and dwell time.Response Agents: Observe the system state and threat scores from the Kafka bus; execute the DQN policy; output mitigation commands to network controllers. Fallback handling is built-in: if the central learning agent is delayed, response agents default to localized rule-based isolation to prevent system failure. 4.8 Operational WorkflowA continuous monitoring of network events occurs at the beginning of the operational workflow. Any suspicious activity is forwarded to the response engine for preliminary analysis by the detection layer. Depending on the evaluated threat level, it may be necessary to reroute the traffic to deception environments or traffic containment may be performed. The dataset contains approximately 2.8 million network-flow records with 78 extracted traffic features and covers both benign and malicious activities. Attack categories represented in the dataset include DoS, DDoS, Port Scan, Brute Force, Web Attacks (SQL Injection, XSS, and Brute Force), Botnet, Heartbleed, and Infiltration attacks. To improve attack diversity and emulate realistic operational conditions, the dataset was supplemented with synthetic traffic generated within the simulation environment. The synthetic traffic included normal user behaviour, authentication events, reconnaissance activities, login attempts, and multi stage attack scenarios. Feature extraction and harmonization were performed to create a unified master dataset suitable for training and evaluation. Prior to model development, preprocessing involved duplicate removal, missing value handling, feature normalization, categorical feature transformation, and extraction of relevant traffic attributes such as packet statistics, flow duration, protocol types, flag counts, login frequency, session behaviour, and temporal traffic patterns. The processed data were divided into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets. Class balancing was performed to reduce bias toward dominant traffic categories and improve detection performance across multiple attack classes.5.2 Experimental Testbed OverviewThe proposed framework was evaluated within a controlled virtualized simulated environment designed to replicate enterprise network operations. The testbed consists of multiple logical hosts representing legitimate users, attacker nodes, defence agents, and isolated deception environments interconnected through software defined network management policies. Public intrusion-detection datasets and synthetically generated traffic were utilized to reproduce realistic operational conditions and diverse attack scenarios. Automated Python-based attack generation scripts were developed to simulate denial-of-service attacks, reconnaissance activities, brute-force authentication attempts, web-application attacks, and infiltration attempts under varying traffic conditions. Network telemetry, authentication events, and system logs were streamed through Apache Kafka and processed by the XGBoost , CNN-LSTM detection pipeline. Response actions were orchestrated through the DQN-based decision engine, while suspicious sessions were redirected to segregated deception environments for intelligence collection. Each attack scenario was executed repeatedly under different traffic loads to evaluate detection accuracy, response latency, deception engagement, and adaptive learning performance.It is critical to note that this framework is fully implemented and operational via real-time Kafka streaming, validating the KPIs experimentally rather than purely conceptually. Implications and Future Applications6.1 Practical Implications cyber threats we face are getting more complicated and that means we need security solutions that can do more than just detect problems. The framework in this study shows how we can use detection, deception that adapts to threats and responses that work on their own to create a strong defense system. This approach can help organizations respond to threats faster understand what is happening better and make things easier for security analysts. The design is also set up to work across parts of a network which makes it more resilient because it does not rely on just one part to make decisions and it lets different parts of the network work together to manage threats. However, autonomous response agents risk generating false positives that block legitimate traffic. To mitigate this, high-impact actions should require 'human-in-the-loop' approvals. Additionally, capturing attacker behavior via honeypots requires strict compliance with privacy frameworks regarding active deception.6.2 Enterprise and Critical Infrastructure ApplicationsThis architecture can be used in different kinds of environments like company networks, cloud computing, smart cities, schools and critical infrastructure systems. Companies with networks that are spread out may really benefit from this framework because it can coordinate many defense systems and adapt to changing attack conditions. Using deception technologies also gives us information about threats that can help us investigate incidents analyze what happened and look for threats before they cause problems.6.3 Future Research DirectionsEven though this framework shows promising results there are still ways we can make it better, in the future. We may be able to use language models and advanced threat intelligence systems to improve our understanding of what is happening during security operations. We can also explore ways for many organizations to work together to improve detection capabilities without sharing information. Also testing this framework in real-world environments would give us more insight into how well it works how well it scales and how well it adapts to real-world conditions over time.7.
